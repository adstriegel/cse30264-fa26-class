# Prompt / Discussion - Application Layer - 09-02-26

## Prompt

What would be most surprising aspects of HTTP or SMTP to an undergraduate student?

## Response - Claude - Sonnet

For most undergrads the shock isn't in the syntax, it's in how little machinery is actually there once you strip away the browser or mail client.

With HTTP, the first surprise is usually how naked it is: no login concept, no session, no memory of you from one request to the next. Everything that makes the modern web feel personalized — being "logged in," a shopping cart that persists, a site remembering your language — is bolted on afterward via cookies, which are just a header convention, not a feature of HTTP itself. The second is that HTTP is plain text, and you can speak it by hand: telnet or netcat into port 80, type "GET / HTTP/1.1" and a Host header, and a real server answers you like a person typing back. Related to that, the "S" in HTTPS is often assumed to be a mode HTTP switches into, when really TLS is a completely separate protocol that HTTP just gets tunneled through — HTTP itself has no concept of encryption, certificates, or identity at all. Status codes surprise people too, once they learn those are honor-system conventions rather than enforced truths — an API can return HTTP 200 with an error message in the body, and nothing in the protocol stops it. And GET vs. POST safety (that GET shouldn't have side effects) is a courtesy developers are supposed to follow, not something the protocol polices; a poorly written server can absolutely delete your data on a GET request. Finally, most students are startled that HTTP/3 drops TCP entirely in favor of QUIC over UDP, since they've been taught to think of "HTTP" and "TCP" as basically inseparable.

SMTP tends to surprise people even more, mostly around trust. The core one: nothing in SMTP verifies who a message is actually from. The "From:" line you see in your inbox is just a header the sender wrote themselves — SMTP will relay it unquestioned, which is why phishing and spoofing were trivial for decades before SPF, DKIM, and DMARC were layered on top as separate, optional add-ons rather than being part of the mail protocol itself. Closely related is that the address your client displays and the address the mail servers actually route on (the envelope sender in "MAIL FROM") can be entirely different — that mismatch is exactly what enables spoofed bounce messages and backscatter spam. Students are also usually surprised that SMTP only pushes mail between servers and has no concept of you retrieving mail to read — that's why POP3 and IMAP exist as completely separate protocols; "email" is really three protocols wearing a trenchcoat. Another one: SMTP was originally 7-bit ASCII only, so anything richer — attachments, images, non-English text — has to be crammed through as Base64 or quoted-printable via MIME, which is why attachments balloon by roughly a third in size; that's a decades-old kludge still running under every email you send. And people are often surprised how minimal the actual command set is — HELO/EHLO, MAIL FROM, RCPT TO, DATA, QUIT — given how enormous and complex the email ecosystem built on top of it feels. Finally, delivery isn't instant or guaranteed: SMTP is a best-effort, store-and-forward relay, so a message can sit queued for hours or silently vanish into a spam trap with no notification to the sender at all.

The throughline in both cases is the same lesson: the protocols are much dumber and more trusting than the polished experience built on top of them suggests, and most of what feels like "the protocol's job" — security, identity, state — actually lives in optional extensions or the applications using it.
